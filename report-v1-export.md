# Executive summary (page 1)

## What is the issue?

LVMH is the world’s largest luxury group.[^1] Its Wines & Spirits arm, Moët Hennessy (Hennessy, Moët & Chandon, Veuve Clicquot), has been 34% owned by Diageo since 1994 and is consolidated in full. Diageo can, at any time and on six months’ notice, require LVMH to buy its 34% for 80% of Moët Hennessy’s fair value. IAS 32 turns that promise into a liability, “Purchase commitments for minority interests’ shares”: €6.3bn at end-2025, down from €11.9bn two years earlier. IFRS does not say where the other side of the entry or the annual revaluation goes. LVMH’s grandfathered choice is goodwill: the liability is re-estimated every year and the whole change goes against goodwill, never through profit.[^2]

## Why is it relevant?

- **The shrinking liability is a write-down in disguise.** It records LVMH’s own valuation of Moët Hennessy falling from about €47bn (2021) to €17bn (2025). The goodwill counterpart went from €9.1bn to €0.4bn, was never impairment-tested, and nothing reached the income statement.

- **Net debt is understated.** Reported net financial debt is €6.9bn. With the put it is €13.2bn, callable on six months’ notice.

- **The booked €4.7bn is 80% of a trough estimate.** Full ownership of Moët Hennessy costs at least €5.9bn at LVMH’s own value; the same stake was valued at €12.5bn in 2023. LVMH switched from peer multiples to its own DCF in 2025 with no explanation and no sensitivity.

- **Earnings are about to become volatile.** In September 2026 the IASB tentatively decided, 12 votes to 0, that remeasurement of such puts goes through profit or loss. On that basis FY2024 net profit would have been €16.9bn rather than €12.6bn.

- **The partner is not friendly and the contract is not disclosed:** a 2020 dividend arbitration, four joint ventures terminated since, and change-of-control terms LVMH does not describe.

## What should we do?

1.  Get the 1994 agreement first: how fair value is set and by whom, notice and change-of-control terms, dividend rules, and the “minority protections” Diageo refers to.

2.  Price the put as debt (€4.7bn booked, €5.9bn at full value, €8–12bn if the cognac cycle recovers before closing), model a €1–4bn remeasurement line in post-deal profit, and carve it out of covenants and earn-outs.

3.  Value Wines & Spirits ourselves. Neither LVMH’s €17bn nor the €13bn implied by Diageo’s books is an audited fair value.

4.  Decide early: keep Diageo at 34%, buy it out in the deal (realistically at 100% of fair value), or sell Moët Hennessy to Diageo. The choice moves the funding need by €5–12bn.

**Proceed, on these terms.** The liability is known, quantified and financeable, and the trough favours a buyer who wants all of Moët Hennessy. It changes the price, not the decision.

# Technical memo (pages 2–4)

## 1. The standard, and LVMH’s policy

IAS 32.23: a contract that obliges an entity to buy its own equity instruments for cash creates a financial liability for the present value of the redemption amount, whether or not exercise is likely. A subsidiary’s minority interest is group equity under IFRS 10, so a put written to a minority shareholder qualifies. IFRS is silent on two things: the debit entry on first recognition, and where later remeasurements go. The IFRS Interpretations Committee proposed remeasurement through profit or loss in 2012 (DI/2012/2); the IASB shelved the draft in 2013 and practice has diverged, French groups reading IFRS 10.23 (ownership changes without loss of control are equity transactions) as allowing remeasurement outside profit. The IASB’s FICE project is re-opening the question: on 22 September 2026 it tentatively decided, 12 votes to 0, that remeasurement gains and losses go to profit or loss, and 9 to 3 that the initial debit is deducted from minority interests, not goodwill. Measurement of the liability and the timetable remain open.[^3]

LVMH’s policy (note 1.13) predates all of this. The commitment is a liability; the corresponding minority interest is cancelled; for commitments granted before 1 January 2010 the difference “is maintained as an asset on the balance sheet under goodwill, as are subsequent changes in this difference”; for later commitments it goes to equity. The Diageo put dates from 1994, so it is the goodwill route. Note 5 lists what is impairment-tested: brands, indefinite-life intangibles and “the goodwill arising on acquisition”. The put goodwill, shown on its own line in note 4, is not among them: it is never tested, only re-plugged each year from the put valuation.[^4]

## 2. What is measured, and where the judgement is

The liability is 34% × 80% of a Level-3 management estimate of the fair value of Moët Hennessy, a private company, at the balance-sheet date, undiscounted because the option is exercisable at any time. Through H1 2025 the estimate applied share-price multiples of comparable firms to Moët Hennessy’s operating results; the FY2025 accounts base it on “Moët Hennessy’s discounted future cash flows, calculated according to the method described in Note 1.16”, the impairment-test DCF.[^5] No reason for the change and no sensitivity is given. That DCF uses, for Wines & Spirits, a 6.9% post-tax discount rate (the lowest of any business group), 5.0% annual revenue growth over the plan and 2.1–3.5% terminal growth (the highest range); the standard sensitivity (1 point on the discount rate, 0.5 point on terminal growth) is disclosed only for Tiffany and TAG Heuer.[^6] Those shocks typically move such a DCF by 15–20%, i.e. €2.5–3.5bn of value and €0.7–1.0bn of liability (€0.27bn per €1bn of value).

Diageo’s books give an independent reading. At 30 June 2026 Diageo carried its 34% at \$5,107m (about €4.4bn, matching LVMH’s cancelled minority interest of €4,369m). Diageo tests the stake at fair value less costs of disposal on an EBITDA multiple, calls it “sensitive to reasonably possible changes in assumptions”, and says a 10% fall in EBITDA would cost \$388m and a 1.0x lower multiple \$312m.[^7] Solving gives a fair value of about \$5.2bn for the 34%, roughly €13bn for 100%, against €17.4bn implied by LVMH’s DCF. On Diageo’s basis LVMH’s liability would be €3.6bn, €1.1bn below book. The partner’s headroom is a few hundred million dollars, LVMH’s number sits at the top of the range, and LVMH changed method in the year that multiples would have given the lowest answer.

Table 1 decomposes the liability: the Diageo put is the goodwill counterpart plus Diageo’s share of Moët Hennessy equity, both disclosed, and dividing by 0.8 and 0.34 gives LVMH’s implied fair value of 100% of Moët Hennessy, about €47bn at end-2021 and €17bn at end-2025. Over 2022–25 the put fell by €7.3bn and €8.7bn of goodwill was written off against it, none of it through the income statement (Figure 1, left). The operating cause is real: Wines & Spirits profit from recurring operations went from €2,109m (2023) to €1,356m (2024) to €1,016m (2025) as cognac demand in the US and China fell away.[^8] Segment intangibles and goodwill fell from €7,775m to €3,512m to €2,423m over the same three year-ends with no impairment line; the CFO’s only comment was that “intangible assets declined due to the decrease in purchase commitments for minority interest”.[^9] Figure 1 (right) shows the IASB’s current direction applied to the past: the goodwill movements become gains and losses and group net profit swings by −20% (2021) to +35% (2024) from a single line.[^10]

## 4. What LVMH discloses, and what it does not

Disclosed: the strike, the notice period, one sentence on the valuation technique, Diageo’s share of profit, equity and dividends, a condensed Moët Hennessy balance sheet (equity €12.6bn, inventories €8.4bn), and the statement that net financial debt “does not include purchase commitments for minority interests’ shares”.[^11] Not disclosed: any sensitivity of the put value, the reason for the 2025 method change, the “provisions for the protection of Diageo as a non-controlling shareholder” that Diageo mentions, and the change-of-control terms: Diageo’s report says LVMH may force a sale of the stake if a competitor takes control of Diageo, while LVMH says nothing about a change of control of LVMH itself.[^12] The put is not a key audit matter; the auditors reach it only through the Wines & Spirits cash flows used for impairment testing.[^13]

*Sources:* FD 2025 pp. 53 (note 19.1), 26, 24 and 40; Diageo put and fair value as Table 1; Diageo basis solved from the \$388m sensitivity in Diageo AR 2026 note 6(a), printed p. 157, at €0.86.

- **Leverage (Table 2).** Reported net financial debt of €6,857m becomes €13,188m with the put, callable on six months’ notice. Liquidity is not the issue (€10.8bn of undrawn confirmed credit lines[^14]); price is.

- **Earnings quality.** Reported net profit is clean of a €30bn swing in LVMH’s own valuation of its drinks arm. If FICE is finalised as proposed, post-deal profit carries a remeasurement line of €1–4bn a year, which covenants, earn-outs and incentives on IFRS net profit must carve out.

- **Cost of owning 100%.** The booked €4,727m is 80% of a trough estimate. Diageo denies any plan to sell and will not exercise at a 20% discount. A negotiated buy-out costs €5.9bn at LVMH’s own fair value and €8–12bn if the cognac cycle recovers towards 2023 levels before closing; if Diageo instead exits at the trough, the acquirer gets the stake at 20% off.

- **Cash and governance.** 34% of Moët Hennessy’s dividends leave the group (€141m in 2025, €241m in 2024),[^15] and the relationship has seen a 2020 arbitration over €181m of withheld dividends and four joint-venture terminations since.[^16]

Does it change the decision? Not on its own: the liability is known, quantified and financeable, and the trough favours a buyer who wants all of Moët Hennessy. It changes the price (€5–6bn is the floor cost of full ownership; value Wines & Spirits on our own numbers) and the sequencing: no signing without the 1994 agreement.

# Use of AI (after the memo, not counted)

Claude (Claude Code) was used throughout, as an analyst rather than as an author. It downloaded and converted LVMH’s Financial Documents, Universal Registration Documents and half-year reports for FY2020–H1 2026 and Diageo’s 2026 annual report; it searched them for the put option and extracted every figure into `data/processed/lvmh_nci_put.csv` with its source and page; and it wrote the Python that recomputes the implied fair values, the pro-forma table and the figures, and the LaTeX that typesets them. It also summarised the standards (IAS 32.23, IFRS 10, the 2012 draft interpretation and the IASB’s September 2026 FICE decisions) and drafted the memo and executive summary text from my notes, which I edited.

Verification: every LVMH figure in the CSV was checked by a script against the text of the cited PDF page (`analysis/verify_citations.py`), and the quotations in the memo were re-read on the page. The Diageo figures and the IASB decisions were checked by hand against the Diageo PDF and the IASB Update. Where the model was unreliable: it initially mis-stated page numbers from markdown conversions that lose page breaks and interleave two-column text, which is why page-level verification was added; and its recollection of the FICE project’s status was out of date and had to be replaced by the September 2026 IASB Update. The conclusions, the choice of issue and the recommendations are my own.
